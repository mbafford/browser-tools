Method. Each episode assumes retirement in January of the start year with your chosen starting balance (default $1,000,000), held for the chosen horizon. The balance compounds by real, inflation-adjusted monthly total returns: price plus reinvested income, in constant dollars. The stocks/bonds slider blends in real total returns for a constant-maturity 10-year U.S. Treasury over the same months. The two sleeves grow independently, and the rebalance control sets how often they reset to your target mix. It defaults to Never, so a crash permanently shifts the mix toward whatever survived. That is why bonds rescue the 1929 window, where they returned +84% real over 1929–1938 against +15% for stocks, but fail in the 1970s, where 1973–1982 gave −18% real for stocks and −18% real for bonds alike.

Sources. Every number in this file comes from one dataset: Robert J. Shiller's “Online Data — U.S. Stock Markets 1871–Present and CAPE Ratio”, the series accompanying Irrational Exuberance (Princeton University Press, 2000, 2005, 2015), published at shillerdata.com and mirrored at econ.yale.edu/~shiller/data.htm. Stock returns are month-over-month ratios of its Real Total Return Price column; bond returns are the same treatment of its Real Total Bond Returns column, which Shiller builds from the 10-year Treasury (GS10) yield series. Inflation adjustment is Shiller's own, using the BLS Consumer Price Index. Nothing is re-deflated, smoothed, or reconstructed here — the monthly factors embedded in this page are those ratios, rounded to six decimals, and each year's row carries its compounded annual return as a comment so it can be checked. Workbook vintage 2026-08-04, retrieved 2026-08-22, covering January 1929 through December 2025.

One caveat that matters. Shiller's monthly S&P price is the average of that month's daily closes, not the month-end close. Cumulative returns over any span are therefore correct, but a mid-month crash gets split across two months: Black Monday (19 October 1987, when the S&P fell 20.5% in a single session) reads here as −12.1% in October and −12.4% in November rather than −21.8% in October alone. Read the timing as accurate to about half a month, not to the day. For the same reason the drawdown quoted on each tile — which is the deepest peak-to-trough of the real total-return index, month-end basis — will not match a figure you remember from a daily chart, and the “deepest month-end” stat card, which starts in January and subtracts withdrawals, is a third number again. Each tile shows both the index drawdown and, where the two differ, the low inside its own danger window.

The horizon slider extends the hold from 10 to 30 years. Each episode uses as much real history as exists, through the end of 2025, so a 1929, 1973, 1987 or 2000 retirement can run its full 30 years on actual returns. Only genuinely future months — in the 2007, 2020 and, past 2030, the 2000-start windows — fall back to the long-run average of these very series (6.64%/yr real for stocks, 1.68%/yr for bonds, computed from the 97 years embedded here), shown as the shaded “projected” region. That part is an assumption, not a forecast.

A withdrawal comes out at the start of every month, before that month's return. In “% of live balance” mode each withdrawal is 1/12 of the annual rate applied to the current balance, so your monthly check rises in good times and shrinks in bad ones. In “Fixed $” mode the dollar amount is locked at the opening balance (for example, 3% of $1M is $2,500/mo for the whole horizon) no matter what the portfolio does — which is the only mode that can empty the account outright, and the stat card will say so when it does.

Illustration only. This is an all-equity buy-and-hold by default, chosen to isolate crash and sequence-of-returns behavior; real portfolios diversify. The bond sleeve is a 10-year Treasury, so it understates what a 20- or 30-year long bond did — notably in 2022, shown here as −20.2% real. Taxes, fees and trading costs are ignored entirely.
